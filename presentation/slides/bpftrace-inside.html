All of it, every start

bpftrace links LLVM and Clang (≈160 MB of libraries in our sandbox) and runs them each time you start a script. Small scripts compile in well under a second, at ≈80 MB RSS.

Great for

Exploring an incident: change the script, run it again, ten times in five minutes.

Before we move on from bpftrace, it helps to know what it actually does when you press enter, because that explains when not to use it. bpftrace is a complete compiler. It parses your script, resolves tracepoint arguments and kernel types from tracefs and BTF, generates LLVM IR, lets LLVM optimise it and emit BPF bytecode, and then does what every loader does: the bpf() syscall, verifier, JIT, attach. When you press Ctrl-C it reads the maps and prints them. You can watch the stages with bpftrace -d all, or keep the IR with --emit-llvm. All of this happens on the host you are tracing, every time you start a script. In our sandbox the bpftrace binary pulls in about 160 MB of LLVM and Clang libraries; a small script compiles in a fraction of a second at about 80 MB resident. That is a great trade for interactive exploration, which is what bpftrace is for.
